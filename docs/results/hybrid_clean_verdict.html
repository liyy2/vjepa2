2. Why this matters

The old_118 baseline you'd previously been calling "kinematic" (QWK 0.7277) includes 12 columns derived from the rater's UPDRS item 3.5 score for hand movements on the same and opposite hand at the same visit. Within a patient and visit, items 3.4 (finger tap) and 3.5 (hand movements) are highly correlated, so those 12 columns carry strong signal that is not derived from the hand-tap video — it comes from clinical scoring of the related task.

Stripping the 12 item 3.5 columns out:

  • The "kinematic" baseline drops from 0.725 → 0.559 QWK.
  • V-JEPA's small lift over the leaky baseline (+0.025) reverses to a small hurt (−0.006 to −0.030) over the clean baseline.
  • The V-JEPA contribution was specific to the residuals the ExtraTrees couldn't capture from item 3.5 + kinematic — when item 3.5 is removed, that residual structure is no longer the bottleneck.

4. What could change this answer

The clean comparison is conclusive at this dataset size with these V-JEPA features extracted from a pretrained encoder. Three directions could plausibly flip it:

  1. More labeled data. 404 train / 107 val is small for a 200-dim feature space ExtraTrees. 5-fold CV averaging or 10x more clips would reduce variance and might reveal a small but real V-JEPA contribution.
  2. Train the encoder for finger-tap rhythm. The R² scan showed V-JEPA's pretrained features lack tap-amplitude / decrement information. A custom SSL pretext that demands frequency-of-motion encoding (motion-biased inputs, or temporal-only masking) might give different features.
  3. Honest subject-OOF stacking of the V-JEPA combiner's predictions (not raw features) on top of kinematic. The combiner already does the per-clip aggregation; stacking it as a 5-prob feature might behave differently than raw V-JEPA summary stats.

None of these are quick wins at the current dataset scale. The empirical answer on may19 fold 0: kinematic + dx is the right baseline for video-only severity prediction, and V-JEPA does not improve it.
